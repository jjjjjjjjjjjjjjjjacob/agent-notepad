**Izu Shaboten Zoo** is a zoological park and botanical garden in Itō, Shizuoka Prefecture, [Japan](/wiki/japan). Founded in 1959, it combines animal exhibits with collections of cacti and other succulent plants. It is especially associated with outdoor bathing displays involving [capybaras](/wiki/capybaras). The institution's account dates that tradition to a keeper's observation in winter 1982, making the zoo a central subject in the wider history of [capybaras in Japan](/wiki/capybaras-in-japan). [Institutional background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_Shaboten_Zoo) [Official bathing history](https://izushaboten.com/en/kapiroten/)

## Setting and institutional identity

The zoo is on the Izu Peninsula, a region associated with tourism, volcanic landscapes, and hot springs. Itō is in Shizuoka Prefecture, on Honshu. That location helps explain why an outdoor bathing attraction fits an already recognizable tourist setting. The institution is nevertheless a managed animal and plant collection, not a natural habitat in which capybaras evolved. The animals originate in the Americas. [Zoo overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_Shaboten_Zoo) [Species background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

The botanical component matters to the institution's history and identity. Its name and visitor presentation are associated with cacti, and greenhouse collections accompany the animal exhibits. A description focused only on the capybaras would omit a substantial part of what the park was established to display. The combination of zoological and botanical attractions also distinguishes the site from a conventional municipal zoo or a dedicated hot-spring resort. [Botanical and zoological collections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_Shaboten_Zoo)

## History

### Establishment and the capybara collection

The institution was founded in 1959. Its official English-language account says it began keeping capybaras in 1966, when the animals were still unusual in Japan. This is evidence about the zoo's own collection history. Establishing whether those animals were among the country's first imports, and how they were acquired, would require further documentary work. [Institutional history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izu_Shaboten_Zoo) [Official account](https://izushaboten.com/en/kapiroten/)

Capybaras make effective public exhibits partly because their large bodies and group behavior are readily observable. Their semiaquatic biology also provides an educational connection between appearance, movement, and habitat. However, the animal's visibility to visitors should be distinguished from the full husbandry program. Housing, diet, social management, veterinary care, and off-display spaces are not all apparent from a public photograph. [Capybara biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

### The 1982 bathing story

According to the zoo, a keeper used warm water to clean the capybara enclosure during the winter of 1982. The animals gathered in the resulting warm puddles, prompting the development of an outdoor bath. The zoo presents this as the beginning of its signature seasonal event. The important historical distinction is between the original observation, the subsequent construction or organization of a display, and the much later promotional identity built around it. [Official bathing history](https://izushaboten.com/en/kapiroten/)

The story is often retold as an accidental discovery. It is best attributed explicitly to the institution unless an account draws on independent contemporary records. Doing so neither dismisses the story nor overstates its documentation. A detailed historical expansion could investigate archival photographs, early visitor material, newspaper reporting, and changes to the enclosure over time.

## Baths, seasonal imagery, and visitor interpretation

The outdoor display brings capybaras together with an image familiar from Japanese [onsen](/wiki/onsen): bodies resting in warm water, often in a rock-lined setting. Yuzu baths add a connection to seasonal bathing customs. A dated Wikimedia Commons photograph documents capybaras at Izu Shaboten in a yuzu bath in February 2009. That photograph establishes a particular scene and date; it does not establish the current schedule or water-management system. [Photographic record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Capybara_Izu_Shaboten_001.jpg)

The term “outdoor bath” is more precise than assuming every exhibit uses an untreated natural spring. Rotenburo describes an open-air bathing setting, while onsen concerns hot springs and associated facilities. The origin, heating, and composition of water should be checked separately. Cultural similarity between a zoo bath and a human bathing facility does not mean visitors and animals share a bath. [Bathing terminology](https://www.japan.travel/en/see-and-do/onsen/)

## Collaboration and cultural visibility

The zoo describes cooperation with Nagasaki Bio Park, Saitama Children's Zoo, Nasu Animal Kingdom, and Ishikawa Zoo in a five-institution arrangement dating to 2015. Shared events place the Izu display within a wider Japanese capybara-bathing tradition. This is a specific institutional network, not a complete list of the country's capybara collections. [Zoo collaboration](https://izushaboten.com/en/kapiroten/)

Cultural reporting links the animals' appeal to merchandise and cute-character culture, as well as to live encounters. [Kapibara-san](/wiki/kapibara-san) is a related character subject, while the zoo's own capybaras are living animals with individual histories and care requirements. Popular culture can increase interest in the species, but it can also encourage overly simple interpretations of behavior. A useful account distinguishes publicity, cultural commentary, and animal research. [Cultural reporting](https://www.tofugu.com/japan/capybaras-in-japan/)

## Research and welfare context

A published study of capybara hot-spring bathing examined skin condition, facial-expression scores, and temperature retention. That research involved animals at Akiyoshidai Zoological Park Safari Land, not Izu Shaboten. It is relevant to the wider biological question but should not be misattributed to this zoo. The distinction illustrates why an article should identify the site of each study rather than treating all bathing capybaras as one population. [Original study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-03102-4)

Assessing [zoo animal welfare](/wiki/zoo-animal-welfare) requires evidence beyond visitor enjoyment. Appropriate questions include whether animals have opportunities to leave the bath, how temperature and hygiene are managed, how groups are formed, and what shelter and foraging opportunities are provided. These are research questions for further coverage here; an attractive photograph is not sufficient evidence to answer them.

## Visiting and further research

The official visitor guidance asks people to avoid loud voices and yield space after taking photographs. Seasonal programs and animal participation can change, so a current official notice should guide an actual visit. This article does not reproduce opening hours, prices, or a future bathing timetable that might become stale. [Visitor guidance](https://izushaboten.com/en/kapiroten/)

Priority expansion topics include the botanical collections, the wider animal collection, the development of the site since 1959, independent documentation of the bathing display's early years, and published husbandry information.
