**Capybaras** are large semiaquatic rodents of the genus *Hydrochoerus*, native to the Americas. The greater capybara, *Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris*, is the largest living rodent. A second living species, the lesser capybara, *H. isthmius*, is smaller and has a more northerly distribution. In everyday writing, “capybara” usually refers to the greater species, which is the main subject of this article. Capybaras are plant-eating, social mammals whose daily lives depend on access to water and adjoining land. Their popular reputation as unhurried or friendly animals captures only a small part of their ecology. [Species reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara) [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hydrochoerus_hydrochaeris/)

## Classification and names

Capybaras belong to Rodentia, the mammalian order that also includes squirrels, mice, beavers, and many other species. Within it, capybaras are cavies, in the family Caviidae. Their close relatives include rock cavies, and their wider family includes guinea pigs. Older literature sometimes places capybaras in a separate family, Hydrochoeridae. Differences in rank and historical classification should not be mistaken for disagreement about whether the animal is a rodent. The capybara and the lesser capybara form the living genus *Hydrochoerus*. [Taxonomic overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

The common name derives from a Tupi expression associated with grass eating. The scientific name has a different linguistic history, drawing on Greek elements for water and pig. Neither the resemblance to a pig nor the occasional comparison to a hippopotamus establishes a close biological relationship. Such comparisons describe appearance or lifestyle; classification is based on evolutionary relationships. [Names and classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

## Body and aquatic adaptations

The greater capybara has a barrel-shaped trunk, a broad head, short legs, and coarse brownish hair. It has no prominent external tail. Adult size varies with age, sex, and population; animals commonly weigh several tens of kilograms. Females may be heavier than males. The large size is unusual among living rodents, but extinct rodents included still larger forms. [Physical description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara) [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hydrochoerus_hydrochaeris/)

The eyes, ears, and nostrils are located high on the head. With the body mostly underwater, the animal can still see, hear, and breathe at the surface. Partly webbed toes aid swimming. Capybaras can also move on land, where they graze, rest, and interact. Water is an essential part of the habitat, but calling them aquatic without qualification would conceal the importance of terrestrial space. [San Diego Zoo](https://animals.sandiegozoo.org/animals/capybara)

Like other rodents, capybaras have incisors that grow continuously and are worn down through use. Their teeth and digestive system support a fibrous plant diet. Coarse hair and skin characteristics also matter in captivity: environmental temperature and humidity differ substantially across facilities, and a seemingly comfortable animal in a photograph may still have specific seasonal care needs. A Japanese study of winter skin condition is discussed under [Capybaras in Japan](/wiki/capybaras-in-japan). [Species reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara) [Bathing research](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-03102-4)

## Ecology

### Distribution and habitat

Greater capybaras occur across much of South America east of the Andes. They inhabit settings such as seasonally flooded grasslands, savannas, river edges, marshes, and forest-water margins. The necessary combination is not simply “rainforest”: suitable water, food, cover, and resting areas can occur in several kinds of landscape. The lesser capybara occurs farther north, including parts of northwestern South America and Central America. Species identity matters when describing range. [Distribution and ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

Access to water allows capybaras to cool themselves, escape threats, and move through their environment. Local conditions and seasonal flooding affect where animals feed and congregate. During dry periods, remaining water and nearby forage can concentrate animals. In wetter conditions, usable habitat may expand. These changing conditions mean that a group size or density reported from one place cannot automatically be applied to the species everywhere. [Ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

Captive populations exist beyond the native range. Zoo displays in [Japan](/wiki/japan), including [Izu Shaboten Zoo](/wiki/izu-shaboten-zoo), are particularly visible online. The popularity of those displays does not imply a Japanese origin, nor does the presence of an escaped individual establish a self-sustaining wild population. Distribution accounts should distinguish native populations, established introductions, isolated sightings, and animals in managed collections. [Capybaras and people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

### Feeding and digestion

Capybaras are herbivores. Grasses and aquatic vegetation are important foods, and the precise diet varies by local availability and season. Grazing shapes both daily activity and the use of space around water. In captivity, feeding should reflect nutritional requirements rather than a visitor's idea of an appealing treat. A photograph of an animal eating fruit does not demonstrate that fruit is the basis of a suitable diet. [Feeding biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara) [San Diego Zoo](https://animals.sandiegozoo.org/animals/capybara)

The animal's digestive strategy includes microbial breakdown of plant material. Capybaras also consume some of their own fecal material, a behavior that helps them recover nutrients from a plant-based diet. This is a normal biological process, not evidence that a particular animal is being neglected. Describing it in its digestive context is more useful than presenting it as an isolated curiosity. Continuously growing teeth, selective grazing, and nutrient recovery all form part of the same feeding system. [Digestive ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

## Behavior and life cycle

### Social life and communication

Capybaras commonly live in groups including adults and young. Group composition and size vary; aggregations may become larger where animals share limited resources. Social organization includes dominance relationships, and males may compete for status and mating opportunities. Descriptions of the species as universally harmonious should therefore be treated as popular shorthand rather than a complete account of its behavior. [Social organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

Communication involves both sounds and scent. Reported vocalizations include alarm barks and several quieter calls. Scent glands and marking behaviors convey information between animals. These channels operate alongside posture, movement, and the relationships already established within a group. An observer who sees several capybaras resting together has seen one part of that social life; interactions at feeding, mating, or disturbance may look different. [Communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

Capybaras are often photographed near birds or other animals. Some associations involve birds using capybaras as perches or feeding around them. Such scenes are interesting ecological observations, but they do not justify a literal claim that capybaras befriend every other species. Individual interactions, the surrounding environment, and the needs of the other animal all matter. [Human descriptions and animal associations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

### Reproduction and development

Mating takes place in water. After a gestation lasting roughly five months, a female gives birth on land to a litter of relatively well-developed young. Pups are mobile early in life and begin sampling vegetation while still nursing. Young animals join the social group rather than spending a long period as helpless nest-bound offspring. These patterns connect reproductive success to both aquatic access and safe terrestrial areas. [Reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

Care and nursing occur within a group setting, and young capybaras may associate with other young animals. Precocial development does not mean that newborns are independent of adults or free from risk. Predation, weather, food availability, and social circumstances remain important. Captive breeding reports should identify the institution and date, rather than treating one litter's size or survival as a universal species norm. [Development and social life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

## Conservation and human relationships

### Predators, conservation, and human use

Capybaras are prey for large predators, including big cats, caimans, and large snakes; the relevant predators vary by habitat and by the age of the animal. Water can provide refuge, but it is not free of predators. Vigilance and alarm communication complement physical escape. Understanding predation helps explain why enclosure shelter and retreat opportunities matter even for captive animals that appear accustomed to people. [Predation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

The greater capybara is widely distributed, and the species reference reports an IUCN classification of Least Concern. That broad category does not mean every local population is secure. Hunting, habitat alteration, and conflicts with agriculture can have local effects. Capybaras have been used for meat and hides, and in some settings they are managed as an economic resource. A conservation assessment should separate global status from local population trends and distinguish dated assessments from fresh field measurements. [Conservation overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capybara)

### Captivity, welfare, and popular culture

In captivity, the central questions concern the complete environment: a suitable social group, space for land-based activity, clean water, nutrition, shelter, and the ability to avoid unwanted contact. The animal's size and ecological needs make it inappropriate to infer pet suitability from calm zoo footage. A downstream [zoo animal welfare](/wiki/zoo-animal-welfare) article is intended to gather formal assessment frameworks and species-specific husbandry evidence.

Japan's capybara bathing tradition provides a particularly well-known meeting point between animal care and cultural presentation. [Onsen](/wiki/onsen) imagery, seasonal baths, and character merchandise contribute to the species' public visibility. The scientific study of bathing examined defined skin and comfort measures in a small captive group; it is evidence about those outcomes rather than a blanket endorsement of every public encounter. [Bathing study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-03102-4)
